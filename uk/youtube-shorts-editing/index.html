What goes into a Short that works

A moment that stands alone

The editor looks for an idea that makes sense without the rest of the video. If the viewer needs context, it is brought forward; if they do not, it is dropped.

A strong first line

A Short competes with the next one in the feed. The opening presents the result, a surprising sentence or the question, then the set-up.

A frame designed for portrait

A 16:9 source is reframed around the speaker or the action and does not simply shrink into a vertical box.

Captions that carry the idea

Accurate captions with a few emphasised words let someone follow with the sound off.

A route back to the channel

A good Short makes people curious about the rest. What you do with links and descriptions stays with you.

The monthly process

A 30-minute call comes first. You describe your channel and your back catalogue, say what you want Shorts to achieve and agree a delivery schedule that fits.

Then the files go into a shared folder: full videos or recordings, a few Shorts you like and your brand fonts and colours. If you already know the best moments, send timestamps; if not, the editor suggests candidates. Finished Shorts return with 2 revisions each, so a changed opening or a shorter ending is a simple request.

Approved Shorts are ready for you to upload. To work out how many to post, read how many short-form videos to post each week.

Sending the right material

Whole recordings beat highlight reels. A two-hour conference session offers a lot more to choose from than the three minutes someone thought were best.

  • The full video or recording at the best quality you have
  • Timestamps for moments you already like
  • Reference Shorts
  • Fonts, colours and logo
  • Claims or quotes that must stay intact

Easy ways to weaken a Short

  • Cutting without the set-up
  • Leaving the slow start
  • Keeping horizontal framing
  • Posting random leftovers
  • Posting once and stopping

In-house, freelance or monthly package

Doing Shorts yourself is realistic for a few, but reframing and captioning twenty takes longer than most people expect. An in-house editor makes sense when a UK business produces video every day, and means salary, employer costs and equipment. Freelancers suit a trial; for a regular feed, the repeated briefing and variation in quality add up.
