What the editor is actually doing

Five parts of the work.

Listening for a self-contained moment

A clip should make sense without the episode around it, so the editor listens through the whole recording instead of searching a transcript.

Tightening without flattening

False starts and long pauses go, but the speaker's natural rhythm stays. That is what makes it feel like a clip and not an excerpt.

Framing the speakers

Wide shots with two or three people become portrait clips that cut between them and keep the person speaking in view.

Captioning every word

Accurate, timed captions that are easy to read on a phone.

Keeping the look consistent

One caption style and palette across clips from different episodes.

How a month goes

Start with a 30-minute call about the show: how many episodes you record, who listens and what you want clips to do. You agree the delivery schedule there.

Then share your logo, fonts, colours and a few clips you like, and upload each episode as it is recorded. Video gives the best clips, and audio-only shows can use simple visuals with strong captions. Clips come back for review with 2 revisions each, and you can mark timestamps you want.

Approved clips are finished files for you to post. For more on the method, read how to turn a podcast into short-form content.

Tips for sending episodes

If guests join remotely, ask them to record their own side locally as well. Cleaner audio and picture make better clips, and the editor can use whichever track is stronger.

  • The whole recording, with video where possible
  • Guest names and the show name
  • Logo, fonts and colours
  • Timestamps you already like
  • Clips from shows you admire

What weakens clips

  • Sending only audio when video exists
  • Taking a punchline without its set-up
  • Letting clips run as long as the topic
  • Leaving off guest names
  • Posting clips only on release day

Do it yourself, hire or subscribe

Cutting your own clips is fine for a handful, but twenty a month alongside recording and promotion is a lot to ask of a host. A producer on the payroll brings wages, on-costs and equipment, which is more than a clip workload of this size needs. A freelancer per episode can help, though each episode needs a new brief.
